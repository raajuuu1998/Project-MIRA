Why Cross-Cancer Generalization Matters in Computational Pathology

Strong performance within one cancer type does not necessarily mean that a pathology model has learned biology that will remain useful when tissue context, patient population, or acquisition conditions change.

From within-cohort performance to genuine biological transfer

Many computational pathology models are developed and evaluated within a single cancer cohort. This is an important first step, but it leaves a fundamental question unresolved: what exactly has the model learned? High internal performance can arise from genuinely disease-relevant morphology, but it can also reflect patterns that are specific to the source organ, institution, staining workflow, scanner, or patient population. When the same model is evaluated in a different cancer, these dependencies are exposed. Cross-cancer evaluation therefore provides a more demanding test of whether a learned representation captures biological information that remains useful beyond the environment in which it was developed.

This challenge extends beyond the technical domain shifts usually considered in cross-site validation. Moving between cancer types also introduces substantial biological variation: tissue architecture, cellular composition, stromal organization, immune infiltration, and tumor morphology can differ markedly across organs. A transferable representation should therefore neither ignore tissue context nor depend entirely on organ-specific appearance. The more interesting objective is to preserve disease-relevant signals that recur across distinct histological environments while allowing genuine cancer-specific biology to remain represented.

Biomarkers as a test of transferable pathology representations

Molecular biomarkers offer a particularly useful setting for studying this problem. Biomarkers such as microsatellite instability and tumor mutational burden arise from different molecular processes and may present differently across cancer types, yet both can be associated with recurring features of the tumor microenvironment, including immune activity and patterns of tumor–host interaction. This raises an important scientific question: can a pathology model identify biological structure that remains predictive when the target cancer has never been used during model development? Answering this requires strict separation between source and target cancers so that performance in the target cohort reflects transfer rather than adaptation.

Evaluating such transfer requires more than a single external score. Multiple target cancers, independent cohorts, confidence intervals, reciprocal transfer experiments, calibration analysis, and component ablations can help determine whether an apparent improvement represents a robust transferable signal or a favorable source–target pairing. Negative transfer is equally informative: if knowledge learned from one cancer improves prediction in one target but harms another, the asymmetry may reveal meaningful biological differences between those diseases. The aim of cross-cancer generalization is therefore not to make every cancer look the same, but to understand which histopathological signals remain accessible and clinically relevant despite substantial changes in tissue context.
